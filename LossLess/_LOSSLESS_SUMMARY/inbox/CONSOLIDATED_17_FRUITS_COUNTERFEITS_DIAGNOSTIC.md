## Working Title

**What Human Beings -- and Human Systems -- Become When They Reward the Opposite**

## Central Thesis

[CLAIM: civilizational diagnostic] A person or society is formed less by what it officially condemns than by what it repeatedly rewards.

The nine Fruits describe mutually reinforcing conditions of durable human coherence.

Their opposites offer faster rewards but generate fragmentation, dependency, distrust, isolation, and instability.

Their counterfeits are especially dangerous because they retain the name of the Fruit while producing the consequences of its opposite.

## The Paper's Distinctive Insight

[CLAIM: counterfeit mechanism] Modern cultures rarely announce that they value betrayal, impulsivity, exploitation, or cruelty.

The anti-fruit normally enters disguised as something good:

- lust calls itself love
- stimulation calls itself joy

## Opening Strategy

Do not begin with a Bible verse and then search for examples that confirm it.

- unprecedented choice alongside weakened commitment

- greater convenience alongside lower patience

Then ask:

> What values, when rewarded repeatedly, would predictably produce these outcomes?

Only after the pattern has been established should the paper reveal that an ancient list already named the opposing behavioral profile.

## Working Thesis

[CLAIM: canon hypothesis, not finished proof] The framework may need fewer root axioms than earlier versions used.

The possible root pair is:

1. God
2. Consciousness

Everything else may be derivative:

- Trinity from God's internal relational life
- truth from God's self-consistency
- distinction from the possibility of knowing
- relation from consciousness encountering otherness
- moral order from orientation toward or away from the Good
- the Fruits from the equilibrium character of life aligned with God

## The Problem

The hard question is:

How do self-proclaiming statements like "I AM" function in the framework?

Are they merely theological claims?

Are they axioms?

Are they revelations?

Are they structural necessities?

The answer may be:

[CLAIM: theological axiom] They are self-declarations of the root.

That means they are not conclusions reached from below. They are not facts discovered by physics and then promoted into theology. They are the ground naming itself.

## Why Self-Declaration Matters

Every system needs something that is not derived from a deeper system.

Mathematics has primitives.

Logic has identity and non-contradiction.

Science has intelligibility, repeatability, measurement, and trust in lawful order.

Consciousness has first-person givenness: before I prove anything, experience is already present.

Theology has revelation: God speaks, names, commands, promises, and discloses.

The question is not whether a system has an underived layer.

Every system does.

The question is whether its underived layer can pay for what the system later spends.

## "I AM" As The Root Naming Itself

[CLAIM: revealed root declaration] The phrase "I AM" is not merely a description.

It is closer to a self-grounding declaration:

> The one who is does not borrow being from another.

If God is the Creator, then God cannot be one object inside reality requiring explanation from a deeper object. God is the ground from which created reality receives being.

That means "I AM" functions differently from ordinary statements.

An ordinary statement points away from itself:

> That tree exists.

The statement depends on a speaker, a language, a world, a tree, and a relation between the words and the thing.

But "I AM," spoken by God, points to the speaker as the ground of being itself.

It is self-declaration, not self-invention.

God does not become true by saying "I AM."

God says "I AM" because He is Truth.

## Truth Before Lie

[CLAIM: structural] There has to be truth before a lie.

A lie cannot create the order it violates. It requires:

- identity
- memory
- meaning
- expectation
- trust
- correspondence
- a hearer
- a world stable enough for the false statement to be false about

So falsehood is parasitic.

It can bend truth, hide truth, counterfeit truth, or weaponize partial truth.

It cannot originate truth.

This matters because the framework should treat truth as non-degradable at the root.

Truth can be obscured in a creature's mind.

Truth can be misrepresented in language.

Truth can be ignored, hated, suppressed, or traded for advantage.

But truth itself does not become false.

## The Number Analogy

[CLAIM: created-order witness, handle with care] The number analogy is useful if handled carefully.

`2 + 2 = 4` is not morally good by itself. It is not personal. It does not save.

But it shows something important:

Some truths do not degrade because a liar abuses them.

Fraud can use arithmetic.

The fraud is corrupt.

The arithmetic is not.

A thief can falsify an invoice, but the wrongness depends on stable number, stable identity, stable comparison, and stable debt. The lie borrows the non-degradable structure of truth in order to deceive.

So the devil cannot corrupt number as number.

He can corrupt the will that uses number.

This may be the deeper pattern:

> Created truths are stable witnesses because their source is not the creature's unstable will.

That is not "numbers are God."

It is:

> Stable truth in creation witnesses to a truthful source.

[CLAIM: theological] The self-declarations of God may function like protected truth-anchors within revelation.

Not protected because no one can misquote them.

- self-grounding
- truthful

- illuminating

- restorative
- victorious over death

[CLAIM: structural/theological] The framework should treat these as theological axioms or revealed root declarations, not as physics-derived conclusions.

## Is There Another System Like This?

There are partial parallels, but not many exact matches.

Mathematics has axioms, but mathematical axioms do not personally declare themselves.

Logic has identity, but identity does not speak.

Descartes has the self-certainty of consciousness, but the self is not the Creator.

Speech-act theory notices that some statements perform what they say, but ordinary human speech acts still depend on prior authority and context.

Christian theology has something stronger:

The ground speaks.

That is the distinctive move.

The root is not merely inferred. The root self-discloses.

In framework language:

> The highest layer is not silent. It declares the identity conditions under which all lower-layer speech becomes possible.

[CLAIM: canon hypothesis] If God is the first axiom, consciousness may be the second because revelation requires a receiver.

Not equal to God.

Part of the reason I care so much about this is because of what I keep seeing happen in online spaces, especially debate rooms where younger people spend enormous amounts of time. Many of them know exactly where Christians are weakest. What hurts me is not that hard questions are being asked. Hard questions should be asked. What hurts me is watching Christians completely collapse under them. Most of the Christians who wander into those conversations have never seriously wrestled with these objections. They have never heard the strongest versions. They have never been taught how to think through them. And the first thing they lose is not the argument. The first thing they lose is their posture. They become angry. Defensive. Fearful. Mocking. Proud. Everything Christians are not supposed to become. I have watched believers who genuinely love God get cornered by questions they were never prepared to answer. And I have watched people use those unanswered questions to walk away from everything -- walk away from the God who loved them, walk away from the gospel that saved them, walk away from two thousand years of witness, transformation, sacrifice, and resurrection -- because of one passage they could not reconcile. So I am not writing this because I enjoy sounding like an atheist. I am not writing this because I want to attack Christianity. I am not writing this because I have lost faith. I am writing this because I think Christians need to learn how to face the strongest version of an objection without falling apart, getting cruel, getting defensive, mocking the person asking, or pretending the question is easy. In parts of this series, I am going to argue the atheist side as strongly as I can. That does not mean I believe the atheist conclusion. It means I refuse to weaken the objection just so the Christian answer feels easier.

> If I soften the argument, I am not doing it justice. If I make the atheist sound stupid, I am not preparing anyone. If I dodge the horror, I am not helping anyone become a faithful witness.

And I want to be clear about something: the answer is not only in the information. The answer is in the posture. The way we respond matters. If we respond with panic, we have already lost something. If we respond with mockery, we have already lost something. If we respond with fake certainty, we have already lost something. If we respond by pretending the verse is not hard, we have already lost something. The first thing a Christian has to do under a question like this is remain Christian. Remain humble. Remain honest. Remain empathetic. Remain reverent. Remain willing to say, "I do not fully know." That may sound weak to some people. I do not think it is weak. I think it may be the first faithful step.

> We are not going to soften the verse. We are not going to pretend it is easy. We are not going to use the pain of the text as an excuse to place ourselves above God. Those three commitments have to stay together.

## Section III: The Objection at Full Force

How do we reconcile the love, grace, mercy, and goodness of God with Old Testament commands where God appears to order the violent destruction of entire people groups? At first, that question can sound abstract. People might say, "God has the right to judge." And maybe that is true. In fact, many Christians, including myself, believe something very close to that. God is the giver of life. God is the sustainer of life. God is the author of life itself. If every breath ultimately comes from Him, then He has rights over life that no human being possesses. That is one of the most common Christian answers. But before we decide whether that answer is sufficient, we should understand exactly what it is trying to solve. Because we are not just talking about God judging evil in a vague theological sense. We are talking about texts where Israel is commanded to invade, destroy, drive out, and kill. We are talking about war. We are talking about blood. We are talking about cities burning. We are talking about bodies in the streets. We are talking about terrified families running from soldiers. We are talking about screams. We are talking about death. And not just soldiers. Women. Children. Infants. Animals. Entire communities. Entire populations.

That is where the question becomes much harder. If the claim is simply "God judged wicked nations," then someone can ask: What about the children? What about the infants? What exactly had they done? Were they personally guilty? Were they morally responsible for the sins of their culture? Or were they swept into judgment because they were born on the wrong side of a divine land promise? And if they were swept into judgment, how do we call that good?

The hard question is not only, "Can God judge?" The harder question is: Can God command something that, if any human being commanded it today, we would immediately condemn as cruel, barbaric, and morally horrifying?

> You Christians condemn child sacrifice among the Canaanites, but then your own Scriptures appear to show God commanding the killing of Canaanite children. You condemn murder, but then say killing can become holy if God commands it. You condemn genocide, but then defend conquest when it is Israel doing it under divine authority. You say God is love, but then you have to explain rivers of blood. You say God is merciful, but then you have to explain infants under the sword. You say God is just, but then you have to explain why the guilty and the innocent seem to fall together.

Now imagine standing there. Not as an Israelite. Not as a theologian. Not as someone who already knows the ending of the story. Imagine standing there as a mother holding a child. Imagine hearing soldiers approaching. Imagine hearing screams from neighboring homes. Imagine hearing steel strike flesh. Imagine watching smoke rise over a city. Imagine watching homes burn. Imagine watching people run. Imagine watching people fall. Imagine knowing that the command was not merely to defeat an army, but to leave nothing alive. Imagine being the child. Imagine being the infant. Imagine being the family that never heard of Abraham, Moses, David, or Jesus. Imagine being on the receiving end of what the victors later called divine judgment.

That is why this objection has emotional power. It is not merely about theology. It is about human beings. It is about fear. It is about terror. It is about grief. It is about death. It is about blood. It is about whether words like justice, mercy, goodness, and love still mean what we think they mean when they are placed beside scenes of conquest, destruction, slaughter, and what appears to be genocide.

I am not saying there is no answer. I am saying the answer has to be strong enough to face the full horror of the objection. Because if we soften the question, we are not really answering it.

## Section IV: The Answers Christians Usually Give

**God Has the Right to Give and Take Life.** God created every human being. Every moment of life is a gift from Him. None of us are owed another day. Because God is the giver of life, He also has the right to take life. Human beings do not have that authority. God does. Many Christians connect this to the idea that every human being has sinned and falls short of the glory of God -- no one is morally innocent in the ultimate sense. What it gets right: It establishes a real distinction between God's authority and human authority. Christianity does not teach that human beings may imitate divine judgment whenever they claim God told them to. Where it runs out: The objection is not merely about whether God has authority. It is about how that authority is exercised. Are infants guilty in the same way adults are? This answer may establish God's right to judge. But it does not fully explain why judgment took this particular form.

**The Nations Were Exceptionally Wicked.** These nations were not ordinary societies. The Amalekites, Canaanites, and others are described as cultures marked by extraordinary evil: violence, idolatry, child sacrifice, sexual exploitation, and deep moral corruption. God was not arbitrarily destroying innocent people. He was judging cultures that had become profoundly wicked. [SCRIPTURE: Genesis 15:16] says the iniquity of the Amorites was "not yet complete" -- centuries of divine patience before judgment. What it gets right: It prevents the conquest from being framed as random ethnic hatred. Judgment is tied to moral corruption, not racial superiority. Israel itself is later judged when it commits the same kinds of evil. Where it runs out: Even if the adults were guilty, what about the children? What about the infants? What about those who had not personally participated in those practices?

**God Was Protecting Israel's Mission.** God was preserving Israel from spiritual corruption. Israel was supposed to be the nation through which God's promises would continue -- the covenant mission, the line to the Messiah. If Israel absorbed surrounding pagan practices, the redemptive plan itself could be threatened. Later biblical history confirms this: Israel repeatedly falls into idolatry after absorbing practices from neighboring nations. What it gets right: It places the command inside the larger biblical story: Israel, covenant, Messiah, redemption, and the eventual blessing of all nations. Where it runs out: Are we saying children died because they might someday become part of a corrupt culture? Are we saying infants became acceptable casualties because of what their society might eventually produce?

**It Was Ancient War Hyperbole.** Ancient Near Eastern warfare often used exaggerated language. Kings claimed they destroyed everyone, left no survivors, wiped out entire peoples -- even when later records showed those same peoples still existed. Phrases like "leave alive nothing that breathes" may function as military rhetoric rather than literal descriptions. Even within the Old Testament, groups described as destroyed sometimes appear again later. What it gets right: It may reduce the scale of the problem in some passages. It reminds us not to read ancient war rhetoric as if it were a modern military report. Where it runs out: Some texts are very specific. In 1 Samuel 15, Saul is condemned because he did not carry out the command completely. The problem in the story is not that Saul was too violent, but that he was not obedient. Hyperbole may help in some places. It does not solve everything.

**The Redemptive-History Argument.** Israel occupied a unique place in history. God was establishing a covenant people through whom the Messiah would eventually come. These events were not ordinary wars. They were part of a unique stage in salvation history. The ultimate goal was not conquest -- it was redemption. The preservation of Israel eventually led to Jesus. And Jesus eventually brought salvation to the world. What it gets right: It keeps the conquest from becoming a general permission structure for religious violence. No modern nation, church, pastor, prophet, president, or army has the right to use these texts as a license for holy war. Where it runs out: Can a redemptive purpose justify the destruction of non-combatants? Can the road to the Messiah run through commands that look, from the outside, like holy genocide? Can a future good justify present horrors?

I do not think any of these answers, by themselves, completely removes the tension. "God has the right to judge" -- true, but that does not answer every moral concern. "Everyone has sinned" -- true, but that does not fully explain infants. "The nations were wicked" -- true, but that does not fully explain collective judgment. "It was hyperbole" -- possible in some cases, but probably not all. "It served redemption" -- perhaps, but that still leaves difficult moral questions on the table. This is not the kind of thing Christians should wave away with "God's ways are higher than our ways." That may be true, but it can also become a shield against moral seriousness. So where do we go? We do not go to another quick answer. We go deeper.

## Section V: Who God Is

Before we can carry this question, we need to go back to the foundation. If someone begins with the blood, the slaughter, the children, the horror, and the command, they are already standing inside the hardest part of the storm with no anchor. And without an anchor, the storm defines God for them. So before we ask, "Why would God command this?" we have to ask a different question entirely. Who is God?

Not merely, "Is God powerful?" Power does not make someone worthy of worship. A tyrant can have power. A demon can have power. An empire can have power. Power alone does not make God.

**God Is Love.** Not love as sentiment. Not love as emotion. Not love as the warm feeling you get when your children are behaving. Love as the fundamental organizing principle of reality. Love that creates at the cost of vulnerability. Love that gives freedom knowing that freedom will be abused. Love that enters the suffering it could have prevented -- not because it was too weak to prevent it, but because prevention would have required destroying the very thing it loved.

**God Is Truth.** Not truth as an attribute He possesses, like a tool in a drawer. Truth as His nature. The reason mathematics works. The reason logic holds. The reason the laws of physics do not change from Tuesday to Wednesday. The entire structure of reality -- every equation, every constant, every law that governs every particle in every galaxy -- rests on a foundation that does not drift.

**God Is Righteousness.** Not righteousness as a rule He follows, as though there were a standard above Him that He is trying to meet. Righteousness as the thing He is. The moral structure of the universe is not something God invented. It is something God radiates. The reason injustice feels wrong to every human being everywhere -- even when they disagree about the details -- is that the moral order is woven into the fabric of what God made, because it is woven into the fabric of who God is.

**God Is the Source of Life.** Every heartbeat. Every breath. Every sunrise. Every cell dividing in every body on the planet. All of it comes from Him. Not in a distant, deistic sense -- not a God who lit the fuse and walked away -- but in an intimate, sustaining, moment-by-moment sense. The universe does not run on its own. It runs because He holds it.

**God Is the One Who Defeated Death.** This God did not stay on the throne. He entered the world He made. He took a body that could bleed. He walked among the people He created. He touched lepers. He wept at graves. He healed the broken. And then He submitted Himself to the worst death the Roman Empire could devise -- not because He was too weak to prevent it, but because the cure for death required going through it. The God who commands judgment is the same God who submitted to judgment. The God who holds authority over death is the same God who died.

**God Can Hold What We Cannot.** No human being can fully hold 100% justice and 100% mercy at the same time. We can lean toward justice. We can lean toward mercy. We can try to balance them. But we cannot perfectly unify them. Too much justice in human hands becomes cruelty. Too much mercy in human hands becomes permissiveness. Only God can hold both without corruption. That is not a theological platitude. That is the structural reality that makes the entire framework hold.

> If you enter the crisis before establishing the character of God, the crisis defines God for you. If you establish God's character first, then the crisis becomes something you bring to God instead of something you use to replace Him.

## Section VI: The Father Who Knows More

If love deepens with knowledge, then God's love is deeper than ours. Imagine a two-year-old child has cancer. She has a six-year-old brother. She has an eighteen-year-old brother. And she has a father. All three love her. All three would grieve her death. But who understands the loss the most? The father. Not because the brothers do not love her. They do. But the father understands more. He understands life more deeply. He understands death more fully.

If God is truly God, then He knows every person more deeply than we ever could. He knows every child completely. Not the way we know a stranger's child whose story we read about -- from the outside, with sympathy but without intimacy. He knows them the way the father knows his daughter. He knows every life from the inside. He knows every possible future. He knows every unlived moment. He knows every laugh that will not happen.

So if knowledge increases grief, then God grieves death more deeply than anyone. Every judgment in the Old Testament was issued by someone who understood its cost more deeply than any human being ever could. Every death God permitted was a death He felt more acutely than the victim's own mother.

> This does not solve the hard verse. But it keeps me from pretending my grief is greater than God's. It keeps me from saying, "If I care this much, God must care less." No. If God is Father, then He cares more.

## Section VII: The Seat You Are Sitting In

The person sitting in the judge's chair believes they are morally clean enough to judge God. They believe the distance between themselves and the horror of that verse is so large that they have earned the right to pronounce verdict. But Jesus said if you have hated someone in your heart, you have already committed murder. Jesus said if you have looked at someone with lust, you have already violated them. Jesus said the outside of the cup can look clean while the inside is full of greed and wickedness. Under that standard, the distance between the questioner and the verse starts to close.

In science, when you find an anomaly -- one observation that does not fit your otherwise successful theory -- you do not immediately throw out the theory. You investigate. You test. You ask whether the anomaly reveals a flaw in the theory or a gap in your understanding. That is epistemic discipline. If someone has encountered the love of God, the truth of Scripture, the transformation of the gospel, the reality of prayer, the witness of creation, the moral order of the universe, the historical resurrection, and the changed lives of millions of people across two thousand years -- and then they hit one verse they cannot reconcile -- what do they do? They can do what bad science does: let one anomaly overthrow the entire framework. Or they can do what good science does: admit the anomaly is real, keep investigating it, and refuse to let one unresolved problem erase everything else they know.

> Be careful when you judge God. Not because the question is forbidden. Because you may not be standing where you think you are standing when you ask it.

## Section VIII: The Three-Constraint Equation

## Section IX: The Story of the King

Three scenes. One king. The whole gospel.

From the throne, you saw his justice -- and it looked severe. From the streets, you saw his heart -- and it looked like love. It was the same king the entire time.

If he holds the sentence, she will die. The king steps down from the throne, removes his robe, unfastens his crown, and stands between his daughter and the executioner. He takes the lashes himself. Three scenes. One king. From the throne -- you see his justice, and it frightens you. From the streets -- you see his heart, and it breaks you. The only God worth trusting is all three.
